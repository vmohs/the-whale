References

There’s a wide range of artists and scholars I have referenced through the creation of The Whale, so I wanted to use this page to pay tribute to them. A lot of my academic research has been based on the writings of trans and queer scholars which has been deeply influential to the way I perceive this work. The Whale is a fundamentally queer work. Queer as in other, as in homo, as in same, as in mirror. The Whale reflects on relationships between trans people as divine subjects, so despite there not being an obvious trans or queer element in the prose, there is still a throughline of gender fuckery, sameness and otherness in interpersonal dynamics and queer monstruosity. Furthermore, I realized I have been shy (for lack of a better word) in sharing my affective experiences about transness. Having trans and queer scholars to reference reminds me that I can and should make unapologetically queer art, that I don’t have to tone myself down in order to pander to cis audiences and that I can be honest about my sacred trans experiences. I have been dividing my sources into academic and creative so I will do the same below:


Academic Sources


The first scholar I’d like to mention is Susan Stryker. Stryker’s essay “My Words to Victor Frankenstein Over The Village of Chamounix” is a work I went back to every couple of weeks while I was working on this project. My Words to Victor Frankenstein highlights the value of the affective trans experience and harboring trans rage as a legitimate place of criticism and theory. I glean something new from this piece every time I come back to it. It offers my project an academic jumping off point and weaves together the ideas of rage, the construction of the body, unapologetic queer living, and the trans body as a divine place through its reclaimed otherness. It’s hard for me to talk succinctly about this essay because there’s just so much for me to unpack there, but I remember the first time I read this a few years ago I realized I had never read something that so deeply described my trans experience and put it into words. It is so valuable to me for such a work to exist and I cannot overstate its importance to The Whale. The seams and sutures within ourselves are the soul of The Whale.


Another trans scholar I’ve been looking to is Paul B. Preciado. I was first introduced to Preciado through their book “Testo Junkie” and was taken aback by the raw, visceral descriptions of transness. They are unapologetically honest, unapologetically erotic. It was a book that made me realize that it is okay to be honest about transness in an academic context, that the knowledge I’ve gained from existing in my trans body is knowledge that can and should be shared, no matter how ugly it may seem. The main work by Preciado I have been referencing for The Whale is “Can The Monster Speak?” which is a transcription of a speech Preciado presented at a psychoanalysis conference. I see it as a sibling source to My Words to Victor Frankenstein because it channels Stryker’s idea of trans rage. We will not allow ourselves to be pushed to the margins. It also added value for me in the fact that it is a transcription of a speech, and the way Preciado dealt with that also helped me in my own transcription of the textile.


Throughout the creation of the data moshed video for The Whale, I also turned to Hito Steyerl. Steyerl is a name that comes up often in Open Media as her way of dealing with video departs greatly from what we think of as “film” or “video art.” “In Defense of The Poor Image” is influential to my practice due to what it manages to put into words. My attraction to the poor image has always been something intuitive, it compelled me for reasons that I couldn’t really put into words. In Defense of the Poor Image spells it out. Hito Steyerl defines the poor image as “a ghost of an image, a preview, a thumbnail, an errant idea, an itinerant image distributed for free, squeezed through slow digital connections, compressed, reproduced, ripped, remixed, as well as copied and pasted into other channels of distribution.” It feels like the broad strokes of where my interest in art lies, in defying Western conventions of art and pushing past what has been labelled as “good,” finding the value in the “wretched of the screen.” The poor image is also similar to the way T4T relationships are constructed for me, as trans people are reproductions of each other. Affected by their own individual context and data information, but existing within this massive stream of connectedness.


Georges Bataille may seem out of place within this list of contemporary scholars, but I have to give credit where credit is due. His book “Erotism: Death and Sensuality” serves as a stepping stone for the more advanced queer theory I am also dealing with. His definitions and frameworks have shaped how I think about eroticism and religion in regards to The Whale, as I perceived queerness and divinity as fundamentally entwined. Bataille’s ideas on the connections between eroticism and the divine have shaped how I approach the religious aspect of my work. Bataille deals with religion in a secular way and throughout the book comes up with various definitions for the ideas of secular religion, eroticism and the threads between both. He also deals with the societal aspects of the taboo and how religious imagery is impacted by this. This source has been very helpful in orientating the religious aspects of my work with the more visceral queer erotics I am dealing with.


Creative Sources

As I have been working on the prose for The Whale, the volume of the text is so large that I found myself thinking about the style of it. I wrote the prose mostly off the top of my head. I wanted it to be honest to my own experiences and vulnerability. Once I stitched something down, I couldn’t take it back. Due to this, I forwent a lot of editing in order to maintain the honesty of the text. I spent a semester in an advanced poetry class refining some of the prose for The Whale into some standalone poems. During the final reading, I felt like my work stood out like a sore thumb. I began to think about why when I realized that the poetry I had been exposed to my entire life, the one that shaped my writing style, wasn’t necessarily what you would define as “poetry” at all.


I realized then that I come from a lyrical background. My writing style feels like the lyrics of a song, especially those of the early 2000s emo scene, that music I love so much had managed to permeate my words. Here is where I mention Pete Wentz, the bassist and lyricist of hit band Fall Out Boy. The way he writes has hypnotized me for very long and my writing style is an ode to him. Growing up with Fall Out Boy songs, I was obsessed with how honest his lyrics were, how abstract yet deeply specific, how he constructs imagery out of the mundane, how he talks about his own relationships. I’ve been going back and reading through his Livejournal blog and realized why my writing doesn’t sound like traditional poetry. It sounds like a confessional diary entry from the 2000s. Full of raw emotion, extremely blunt and confrontational, addressed to a specific reader and not to an audience, situated in a specific time and place. I’m citing Infinity on High as the album I have looked to the most throughout the creation of The Whale, mostly because of a specific lyric in Hum Hallelujah: “I love you in the same way there’s a chapel in a hospital.” This feels like the thesis statement for The Whale.


Apart from lyricism, there is also some poetry I have been looking toward with this project. Namely “Syzygy, Beauty: An Essay” by T. Fleischmann and “Crush” by Richard Siken. Both of these books are extremely queer, in Syzygy’s case extremely trans. Crush was the first book of queer poetry I ever read. It’s the book that made me realize the value of queer poetics through how seen I felt while reading it. Its raw, unapologetic queerness is something I try to emulate in my work. Not only the beauty of being queer, but the pain of it. Syzygy, Beauty felt life changing for me in a similar way. The way it deals with transness and polyamory felt so honest and sacred. Positing queer bodies as celestial bodies helped frame my interest in queerness and divinity and helped me find words to communicate the things that I felt in my body about the connections between holiness and queer relationships and structures.
